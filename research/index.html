Regulatory Friction and Market Exit in the California Homeowners Insurance Market
California's Proposition 103 requires prior approval of homeowners insurance rate changes, creating a wedge between actuarial risk and permitted prices. I develop a three-stage structural model in which insurers (i) file state-level rates subject to a regulator's approval technology with time-decay, (ii) make zip-code-level participation decisions, and (iii) adjust portfolios at the margin via continuous net policy flow. Identification of the regulatory cost parameter comes from GMM moments built around a 2019 wildfire moratorium interacted with insurer-specific catastrophe model sophistication. I find that regulatory friction explains a substantial share of post-2017 exit and coverage retreat in high-wildfire ZCTAs, with sharper effects among insurers using Granular CAT models in the sense of Boomhower, Fowlie, Plantinga, and Wibbenmeyer (2024).

Targeted Selection in Homeowners Insurance Menus: Evidence from Deductible and Price Factor Structures
with [Coauthor]
Draft
Using rate filings from California's CDI, we construct two measures of targeted selection — the Deductible–Price Factor Gradient (DPG) and the Menu Dispersion Index (MDI) — and test their predictions for exposure runoff in wildfire-prone ZCTAs. Both measures predict steeper exposure decline in high-risk areas, consistent with insurers using menu design to deselect risk under binding rate regulation.
